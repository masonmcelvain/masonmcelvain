iFixit calls itself the [world's largest online repair community](https://www.ifixit.com/Info). It publishes thousands of free repair guides, so you can swap your phone's battery yourself, and it sells the parts and tools you need to do it.

I work on that store. For almost six years I've built the pages where you shop for a part, the checkout where you pay for it, and the plumbing that gets your order from a website to a warehouse. It's my first job as a software engineer, and so far my only one.

It started with a take-home interview project in October 2020, while I was still a computer science student at Cal Poly. I joined that December, part-time.

## How it started

The interview project was [iFixit Grab Bag](https://ifixit-grab-bag-mm.netlify.app/), a small React app I built in six days on iFixit's public API. You drag the devices you own into a "bag," it remembers them, and you can jump straight to their repair guides. The README signs off with "Thanks for your interest, happy hacking!"

Five weeks later, on December 1, 2020, I opened my first pull request. It removed an old Apps page that pointed to apps that no longer worked: one line added, 266 deleted. I didn't know it yet, but deleting things would become a theme.

For my first year and a half I worked around classes, and you can read the school calendar in my history. A handful of pull requests a month during the quarter, a spike the summer I worked full-time hours, and just five in April 2022, my last spring at Cal Poly. After graduation I went full-time, and the pace never dropped back. The busiest month so far was October 2024, with 78.

Two months in, I opened what is still my most-discussed pull request: 61 comments over 39 days, about what our records should do when someone removes an item from an order. Its testing instructions walked reviewers through creating a test order, editing it in Shopify, and checking the database by hand. It was a crash course in how a real team reviews code.

<Callout title="What I learned">
   Write the pull request for the person reviewing it. Spell out what changed,
   why, and exactly how to check it, and a long review stops being a fight and
   starts being a conversation.
</Callout>

## Checkout and the order pipeline

When you buy a part on ifixit.com, Shopify takes your payment, but iFixit also keeps its own records of orders, customers and inventory. Starting in 2021, I wrote much of the PHP that keeps the two in sync: webhooks and scheduled jobs that copy orders and customers across, handle edits, cancellations and returns, and admin dashboards and command-line tools for whoever has to fix things when they go sideways.

Money code is unforgiving. In December 2021 I rewrote how we compute order subtotals, building them from each line's final price. The pull request notes that it "also corrects an off-by-one-cent bug" on percentage discounts for cancelled orders. One cent sounds harmless until the books have to balance.

In 2023 iFixit started a Shopify checkout app, and it's since collected 221 of my pull requests. It asks EU business customers for a VAT ID, hides shipping methods that can't carry batteries, applies discounts for Pro customers, and reports what happened through web pixels for analytics. Much of that runs as Shopify Functions, small programs Shopify runs inside its own checkout. In April 2024 I wrote the same Function twice, once in JavaScript and once in Rust compiled to WebAssembly, in a pull request that was never meant to merge. It existed "to provide a medium for us to compare the toolchains."

## The storefront

In 2022 iFixit rebuilt its store pages, like [Parts](https://www.ifixit.com/Parts) and [Tools](https://www.ifixit.com/Tools), as a new site. For the technical folks: Next.js on Vercel, with Strapi as the CMS, Algolia for search, and Shopify's Storefront API for products and carts. The code is public as [react-commerce](https://github.com/iFixit/react-commerce). I started there in May 2022 and sent 108 pull requests that year, on search filters, SEO, analytics, and the hreflang tags that tell search engines which page is for which country.

Not all of them went well. In October 2022 I shipped a middleware meant to hide our development sites from search engines, and it started hiding www.ifixit.com/Parts instead. I [reverted it the same day](https://github.com/iFixit/react-commerce/pull/778) with a one-line explanation, "This is noindexing www.ifixit.com/Parts, yikes!", and shipped the real fix the next day.

By June 2023 the [Store](https://www.ifixit.com/Store) homepage was served by Next.js. In 2024 I built a Shopify cart package and brought the Next.js site's cart drawer into the older PHP site, so your cart looks the same no matter which half of ifixit.com you're on.

## The great deletion

Late in 2023, iFixit moved order management and inventory to NetSuite, an off-the-shelf business system. I wrote the code that sends our products to NetSuite, the sync that pulls its inventory and orders back, and the cutover switch that handed orders over in November 2023.

Then came the fun part. In January 2024 I opened more than 20 pull requests whose titles start with "Delete," removing some 20,000 lines of in-house refunds, returns, payment capture, shipping webhooks and inventory tools. Some of it was code I'd helped build as a student. Watching it go felt less like loss and more like finally putting tools back in the drawer.

<Callout title="Subtraction is progress">
   The best code to maintain is code you no longer have. A migration isn't done
   when the new system works. It's done when the old one is gone, and nobody has
   to remember which of the two is telling the truth.
</Callout>

## Many countries, many languages

iFixit sells in Europe, Australia and Canada from separate Shopify stores, and publishes in a dozen languages. My largest pull request by far was catching the [EU](https://eu.ifixit.com/) Pro store's theme up with the main theme in 2022: 70,940 lines added. My longest-running one, automatically publishing the EU stores, stayed open for 150 days in 2023 before it merged.

Since then I've worked on VAT validation, hreflangs everywhere, a soft launch of the [Italian store](https://it.ifixit.com/Store) in May 2025, moving the storefront's text into translatable strings, and automating the flow of translations through Crowdin with a CI check for consistency. In 2026 I brought a single location picker into the site header, so you can choose your country and language in one place, along with a dashboard to watch it roll out.

<Callout title="What I learned">
   Every country is a new edge case. Currency, tax, and even the wording of a
   warranty change at the border, so the job is less about translating text and
   more about making the right rules easy to find.
</Callout>

## Reviewing more than I write

I've reviewed 2,508 of my teammates' pull requests, more than the 2,268 I've opened. It has climbed from 4 in my first December to 628 so far in 2026. Reviewing is how I learn the corners of the codebase I don't touch, and how I pay forward that 61-comment welcome.

Not everything I write is code. In May 2026 I opened an RFC, a pull request with nothing in it but a markdown document, so the team could agree on how to decide which language and country to show a visitor before anyone wrote a line.

For a couple of years a teammate sent me CSV files of compatibility data, which parts fit which devices, and I ran them through a script for them. In January 2026 I built an admin page so they could import them themselves. Lately I've been writing the rules that let AI coding agents work in our codebase the way we do.

And I still make mistakes, in public. My pull request descriptions include "Oops," one honest ":facepalm:," and a redo of a change "I mistakenly merged then reverted."

<Callout title="Make yourself unnecessary">
   The most useful thing I can build for a teammate is often a tool that means
   they don't need me. Fewer handoffs, fewer messages, and more time for the
   work only people can do.
</Callout>
